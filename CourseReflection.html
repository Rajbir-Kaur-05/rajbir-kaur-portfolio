Course Reflection

I respectfully acknowledge that I work, play, and learn on the shared, traditional, ancestral, and unceded territories of the Katzie, Semiahmoo, and Kwantlen First Nations.

01

Reflecting on Learning Goals and Ongoing Learning

At the beginning of this course, I identified three learning goals that I hoped would strengthen both my understanding of Indigenous education and my future teaching practice:

  1. Enhance my knowledge of Indigenous histories, worldviews, ways of knowing, and the impacts of colonization so that I could better support Indigenous learners and contribute to reconciliation in education.
  2. Know how to integrate Indigenous learning approaches, strategies, and resources in my own teaching.
  3. Develop an environment for learning where all the learners will feel included and respected, thus gaining more understanding and appreciation of the Indigenous peoples and their culture

Looking back at my introduction from Module 1, I can see how much my thinking has evolved. At the beginning of the course, I recognized the importance of Indigenous education, but my understanding was limited primarily to learning about historical events and incorporating Indigenous content into the classroom. Through the course, I have come to understand that reconciliation requires far more than knowledge alone. It involves building respectful relationships, engaging in continuous self-reflection, challenging colonial assumptions, and creating learning experiences that honour Indigenous voices, perspectives, and ways of knowing throughout everyday teaching. Although I have made meaningful progress toward my goals, I also recognize that becoming a culturally responsive educator is a lifelong journey. As I approach the end of the course, I continue to reflect on several important questions that will guide my future professional growth.

Evidence of My Learning Growth

Reflecting on my work throughout the course demonstrates how my learning has moved me toward achieving each of my original goals.

02

Goal 1:

Enhance my knowledge about Indigenous histories, worldviews, ways of knowing, and the impacts of colonization.

Throughout my reflections and discussion posts, I developed a much deeper understanding of Indigenous histories and contemporary realities. Learning about treaties, the Indian Act, residential schools, the Sixties Scoop, self-government, UNDRIP, DRIPA, and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Calls to Action helped me recognize the ongoing impacts of colonization on Indigenous communities. My understanding also expanded through exploring Indigenous worldviews, Two-Eyed Seeing, and the First Peoples Principles of Learning, which challenged me to appreciate multiple ways of knowing rather than viewing Western perspectives as the only approach to education. This growth is evident throughout my weekly reflections, where my thinking progressed from building awareness to critically examining how colonial systems continue to influence education today

03

Goal 2:

Know how to integrate Indigenous learning approaches, strategies, and resources into my own teaching.

Evidence of my growth can be seen through the lesson plans, inquiry projects, assessment tools, and collaborative discussions I completed during the course. I intentionally incorporated authentic Indigenous resources, storytelling, inquiry-based learning, land-based education, and the First Peoples Principles of Learning into my classroom planning. Developing a cross-curricular inquiry project and designing lessons that applied Two-Eyed Seeing helped me understand that Indigenous and Western ways of knowing can complement one another to create richer learning experiences for all students. Feedback from classmates and collaborative discussions also encouraged me to think more critically about avoiding tokenism and ensuring Indigenous perspectives are integrated throughout the school year rather than during isolated events.

04

Goal 3:

Develop an environment where all learners feel included and respected while gaining a greater understanding and appreciation of Indigenous Peoples and their cultures.

Throughout my reflections and collaborative activities, I increasingly recognized that culturally safe classrooms are created through respectful relationships, authentic representation, and ongoing reflection on my own teaching practices. My learning about cultural safety, reconciliation, and decolonization reinforced the importance of creating spaces where Indigenous students feel valued and where all learners develop empathy, respect, and an appreciation for Indigenous cultures and knowledge systems. Discussions with classmates further strengthened my understanding that reconciliation is built through everyday classroom decisions, including selecting authentic resources, honoring Indigenous voices, and creating opportunities for meaningful dialogue. These experiences have increased my confidence in creating inclusive learning environments while reminding me that this work requires continuous learning and self-reflection.

05

Three Lingering Questions

1. How can I continue building authentic relationships with local Indigenous communities, Elders, and Knowledge Keepers in a respectful and sustainable way?

This question continues to resonate with me because one of the most valuable lessons I learned throughout the course is that authentic Indigenous education is built on relationships rather than resources alone. Meaningful reconciliation cannot occur if educators rely only on textbooks, websites, or prepared lesson plans. Indigenous knowledge is connected to the people, the land, language, and community. I realize that respectful relationships require time, trust, humility, and a willingness to learn from others rather than assuming I already have the answers. As an educator, I want to understand appropriate cultural protocols, learn from local Nations, and ensure that Indigenous perspectives in my classroom are grounded in authentic community connections rather than generalized information.

2. How can I authentically integrate Indigenous perspectives across all subject areas without falling into tokenism?

This question remains important because one of the greatest shifts in my thinking has been recognizing that Indigenous perspectives should be woven naturally throughout teaching instead of being limited to special events or isolated lessons. Earlier in my teaching journey, I believed that acknowledging National Indigenous Peoples Day or teaching about residential schools fulfilled my responsibility. This course challenged that belief by showing me that reconciliation is reflected in everyday teaching practices. I want to continue learning how to integrate Indigenous perspectives purposefully across mathematics, science, English language arts, and social studies while ensuring that Indigenous knowledge is presented respectfully, accurately, and meaningfully. This question encourages me to continually reflect on my teaching choices and avoid treating Indigenous perspectives as an "add-on" to the curriculum.

3. How can I help students learn about the difficult histories of colonization while also recognizing Indigenous resilience, strengths, and contemporary contributions?

This question continues to guide my thinking because I now understand that Indigenous education should present a balanced and truthful perspective. Students need to learn about the impacts of colonization, residential schools, and systemic injustices to understand why reconciliation is necessary. At the same time, I do not want Indigenous Peoples to be viewed only through narratives of trauma. Throughout this course, I learned about Indigenous leaders, artists, healthcare professionals, athletes, Knowledge Keepers, self-government, and community leadership that demonstrate resilience, innovation, and strength. As a teacher, I want my students to understand both the historical truths and the vibrant, living cultures that continue to shape Canada today. This balance will help students develop empathy, respect, and a deeper appreciation for Indigenous knowledge and contributions.

06

Final Reflection

Reflecting on my learning journey, I can confidently say that this course has deepened both my knowledge and my perspective as an educator. It has challenged me to think critically about my role in reconciliation and has shown me that meaningful Indigenous education is built through respect, authentic relationships, continuous learning, and thoughtful action rather than through isolated lessons or activities. I now have a much stronger understanding of Indigenous histories, worldviews, and ways of knowing, as well as a greater appreciation for the importance of listening to Indigenous voices and learning from Indigenous communities.

As I move forward in my teaching career, I am committed to continuing this learning journey with humility and an open mind. I recognize that reconciliation is an ongoing responsibility that requires reflection, collaboration, and a willingness to keep learning from mistakes. I will continue to seek authentic resources, build respectful relationships with local Indigenous communities whenever possible, and thoughtfully integrate Indigenous perspectives across all areas of the curriculum. My goal is to create a classroom where every student feels respected, included, and encouraged to appreciate the diverse histories, cultures, and knowledge systems that enrich our communities. This course has provided me with a strong foundation, and I am motivated to continue growing as an educator who contributes meaningfully to reconciliation through everyday teaching practice.
