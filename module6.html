01

Decolonization and Indigenization

Learning

From this learning, I gained a deeper understanding that decolonization and Indigenization are connected but have different purposes. I learned that decolonization involves questioning and changing colonial systems, policies, and ways of thinking that continue to influence education, while Indigenization focuses on respectfully embedding Indigenous knowledge, worldviews, languages, cultures, and pedagogies throughout teaching and learning (Queen's University Centre for Teaching and Learning [CTL], n.d.). I also learned that both processes require authentic relationships with Indigenous communities, continuous self-reflection, and a long-term commitment to creating equitable and culturally responsive learning environments rather than making symbolic or one-time changes (Kulago et al., 2023; Sanchez, 2019).

Why I Selected This

I selected this learning because it helped me clearly understand the difference between decolonization and Indigenization and how both are essential for meaningful reconciliation in education. Before studying this topic, I thought that including Indigenous content in lessons was enough. This comparison showed me that true reconciliation requires examining my own teaching practices, challenging colonial assumptions, valuing Indigenous knowledge equally alongside Western knowledge, and building respectful relationships with Indigenous communities. It reinforced that becoming a culturally responsive educator is an ongoing journey of learning and reflection.

How I Will Apply This in My Classroom

  • Integrate Indigenous perspectives, histories, and ways of knowing across different subject areas instead of teaching them as isolated topics.
  • Use authentic Indigenous resources created or recommended by Indigenous authors, organizations, Elders, and Knowledge Keepers.
  • Encourage students to examine multiple perspectives and think critically about history, power, equity, and reconciliation.
  • Create a classroom environment where Indigenous students feel respected, represented, and included.
  • Build respectful relationships with local Indigenous communities whenever appropriate to support meaningful learning experiences.
  • Reflect on my own teaching practices regularly to ensure I am moving beyond symbolic inclusion toward meaningful reconciliation.
02

Potlatch as Pedagogy

Learning

From this learning, I discovered that education is not only about gaining knowledge but also about building relationships, strengthening community, and learning through shared experiences. The story of the 1969 Haida pole raising taught me that knowledge is passed down through participation, ceremony, observation, and relationships rather than through individual achievement alone. The teaching, "That pole doesn't belong to you anymore," reminded me that knowledge and accomplishments are gifts to the community and that everyone has a responsibility to contribute to and preserve culture (Davidson & Davidson, 2018). This learning also reinforced the importance of collaboration, humility, reciprocity, and collective responsibility in education.

Why I Selected This

I selected this learning because it changed my perspective on what meaningful education looks like. Before reading this chapter, I often thought of learning as something individuals achieve through their own efforts. This teaching helped me understand that learning is deeply relational and that students grow best when they learn together, support one another, and contribute to their community. It also strengthened my appreciation for the First Peoples Principles of Learning, which emphasize relationships, shared responsibility, experiential learning, and respect for community knowledge.

How I Will Apply This in My Classroom

  • Create collaborative learning experiences where students learn from one another and recognize that everyone's contributions strengthen the classroom community.
  • Use storytelling, inquiry, and hands-on activities to encourage learning through participation and shared experiences.
  • Incorporate the First Peoples Principles of Learning by emphasizing relationships, respect, reflection, and experiential learning.
  • Foster a classroom culture where students value cooperation, generosity, and collective responsibility rather than individual competition.
  • Invite local Indigenous Elders or Knowledge Keepers, when appropriate, to share their knowledge and cultural teachings.
  • Encourage students to reflect on how their own strengths and actions can positively contribute to the learning community, reinforcing that learning is a shared responsibility.
03

Promoting Cultural Safety

Learning

From this learning, I gained a deeper understanding that cultural safety is about creating learning environments where Indigenous students and families feel respected, valued, and supported. I learned that cultural safety requires more than including Indigenous content in the curriculum - it involves building trusting relationships, recognizing the ongoing impacts of colonization and systemic racism, and ensuring Indigenous identities, cultures, and perspectives are meaningfully represented throughout the school year (National Collaborating Centre for Indigenous Health [NCCIH], 2017). I also learned that creating culturally safe classrooms requires educators to continually reflect on their own assumptions, challenge power imbalances, and commit to lifelong learning and reconciliation (Pierre, 2021).

Why I Selected This

I selected this learning because it helped me recognize that creating a culturally safe classroom is an essential responsibility for every educator. Before exploring this topic, I understood the importance of inclusion, but I had not fully considered how historical and ongoing experiences of colonization may affect Indigenous students' sense of belonging at school. This learning reminded me that meaningful reconciliation begins with building respectful relationships, listening to Indigenous voices, and creating classroom environments where every student feels safe, represented, and valued. It also reinforced that cultural safety is an ongoing practice rather than a one-time initiative.

How I Will Apply This in My Classroom

  • Build trusting relationships with students and families by listening respectfully and valuing their experiences and perspectives.
  • Create a welcoming classroom where Indigenous students see themselves represented through authentic books, resources, displays, and learning activities throughout the year.
  • Integrate Indigenous perspectives across different subject areas using authentic resources created or recommended by Indigenous authors, organizations, Elders, and Knowledge Keepers.
  • Incorporate land-based learning experiences that help students develop respectful relationships with the land and Indigenous ways of knowing.
  • Establish classroom expectations based on respect, kindness, empathy, and inclusion so that every student feels safe and has a sense of belonging.
  • Continue reflecting on my own teaching practices and learning about Indigenous histories, cultures, and cultural safety to support meaningful reconciliation and culturally responsive teaching.
04

Inquiry Question Update

Inquiry Question

How can I authentically integrate Indigenous perspectives and Two-Eyed Seeing across the BC elementary curriculum to support meaningful reconciliation?

How This Module Expanded My Thinking

Module 6 helped me realize that authentically integrating Indigenous perspectives is not only about including Indigenous content but also about transforming how students learn. Learning about decolonization and Indigenization, Potlatch as Pedagogy, and cultural safety showed me that meaningful reconciliation requires building respectful relationships, valuing Indigenous knowledge alongside Western knowledge, creating culturally safe learning environments, and embedding Indigenous perspectives throughout everyday teaching rather than treating them as add-ons (Queen's University Centre for Teaching and Learning, n.d.; Davidson & Davidson, 2018; National Collaborating Centre for Indigenous Health, 2017). As an educator, I want to create inquiry-based, relationship-focused learning experiences that honour Indigenous voices, encourage collaboration, and help all students develop respect for Indigenous histories, cultures, and ways of knowing while supporting meaningful reconciliation.

05

Overall Reflection

Module 5

Building on my learning from the first five modules, Module 6 expanded my understanding of what it means to be an educator who supports reconciliation through meaningful action. Earlier modules helped me understand Indigenous worldviews, the impacts of colonization, and Indigenous resilience, while this module showed me how those understandings can be reflected in everyday teaching practices. I now recognize that reconciliation is not a single lesson or activity, but an ongoing commitment to learning, reflection, relationship-building, and creating culturally responsive learning environments. Before taking this course, I believed supporting reconciliation mainly meant teaching Indigenous history. Through the first six modules, I have come to understand that reconciliation also involves transforming how we teach, building authentic relationships, and creating culturally safe learning environments.

My first key takeaway was learning about decolonization and Indigenization. I learned that these concepts are connected but have different purposes. Decolonization challenges colonial systems, assumptions, and inequities, while Indigenization respectfully embeds Indigenous knowledge, perspectives, and pedagogies throughout education. This learning helped me realize that meaningful reconciliation requires more than adding Indigenous content to lessons—it requires transforming teaching practices, valuing Indigenous knowledge equally, and building authentic relationships with Indigenous communities (Queen's University Centre for Teaching and Learning, n.d.; Kulago et al., 2023).

My second key takeaway came from Potlatch as Pedagogy: Learning Through Ceremony. The story of the 1969 Haida pole raising showed me that education is grounded in relationships, community, and shared responsibility. The teaching, "That pole doesn't belong to you anymore," reminded me that knowledge is meant to benefit the whole community rather than individuals alone. This challenged me to think differently about classroom learning by valuing collaboration, generosity, and collective success alongside individual achievement (Davidson & Davidson, 2018; First Nations Education Steering Committee [FNESC], n.d.).

My third key takeaway focused on promoting cultural safety. I learned that cultural safety goes beyond inclusion and requires creating classrooms where Indigenous students and families genuinely feel respected, represented, and valued. This learning emphasized the importance of building trusting relationships, using authentic Indigenous resources, reflecting on my own assumptions, and recognizing the ongoing impacts of colonization and systemic racism. It also reinforced that cultural safety is an ongoing responsibility that requires humility, continuous learning, and meaningful action (National Collaborating Centre for Indigenous Health [NCCIH], 2017; Pierre, 2021).

Overall, the learning from the first six modules, especially Module 6, has strengthened my commitment to becoming a culturally responsive educator. I now understand that reconciliation is reflected in everyday teaching practices through authentic relationships, respectful dialogue, inquiry, collaboration, and ongoing self-reflection. As a teacher in British Columbia, I want to create a classroom where Indigenous students feel a strong sense of belonging and where all students develop respect for Indigenous histories, cultures, knowledge systems, and ways of knowing. By continuing to learn from Indigenous voices and incorporating these teachings into my practice, I hope to contribute to a learning environment that is inclusive, equitable, and supportive of meaningful reconciliation.
