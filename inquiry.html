Inquiry Project

Authentically Integrating Indigenous Perspectives and Two-Eyed Seeing Across the Curriculum to Support Meaningful Reconciliation

I respectfully acknowledge that I work, play, and learn on the shared, traditional, ancestral, and unceded territories of the Katzie, Semiahmoo, and Kwantlen First Nations.

01

Introduction

Education plays an essential role in advancing reconciliation by helping students develop truthful understandings of Canada's history while building respectful relationships with Indigenous Peoples. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada (TRC, 2015) identifies education as a key pathway toward reconciliation, emphasizing the need for students to learn about Indigenous histories, cultures, rights, and contributions. As educators, our responsibility extends beyond teaching historical facts; we must create classrooms where Indigenous perspectives are respected as valuable ways of knowing and learning.

Before beginning this course, I believed that supporting reconciliation meant teaching about residential schools, Indigenous cultures, and important historical events during specific units or commemorative days. Although these topics remain important, I now understand that limiting Indigenous perspectives to isolated lessons can unintentionally reinforce tokenism. Authentic reconciliation requires educators to critically examine their teaching practices, build respectful relationships with Indigenous communities, and integrate Indigenous perspectives throughout everyday learning (Chrona, 2022).

This inquiry explores the question:

How can I authentically integrate Indigenous perspectives and Two-Eyed Seeing across the curriculum to support meaningful reconciliation?

Throughout this course, I explored Indigenous worldviews, colonial history, educational policies, Indigenous pedagogies, and approaches to reconciliation. These experiences challenged many of my assumptions about teaching and helped me understand that reconciliation is an ongoing responsibility rather than a single initiative. Central to this inquiry are Two-Eyed Seeing (Etuaptmumk), the First Peoples Principles of Learning, decolonization, Indigenization, land-based learning, cultural safety, Indigenous self-determination, UNDRIP, British Columbia's Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act (DRIPA), and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Calls to Action, particularly Call to Action 62 (Government of British Columbia, 2022; TRC, 2015; United Nations, 2007).

As an educator, I have also come to recognize the importance of presenting Indigenous Peoples not only through the history of colonization but also through their resilience, leadership, and ongoing contributions to Canadian society. Learning about Indigenous educators, artists, healthcare professionals, authors, and Knowledge Keepers challenged deficit-based narratives and reminded me that Indigenous knowledge continues to shape education, science, governance, and community life today (National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation [NCTR], 2019). This shift has strengthened my commitment to creating an inclusive classroom where Indigenous perspectives are embedded across the curriculum in meaningful and respectful ways.

02

Initial Understanding and Inquiry Journey

When I began this course, I approached Indigenous education with respect but limited knowledge. Having completed my education outside Canada, I knew little about treaties, residential schools, the Indian Act, the Sixties Scoop, or Indigenous governance. I initially believed that integrating Indigenous perspectives meant adding Indigenous books or activities to existing lessons. Throughout this course, I realized that this approach can become tokenistic because it treats Indigenous knowledge as an addition rather than an equally valuable way of knowing.

A major turning point was learning about the First Peoples Principles of Learning (FPPL), which emphasize that learning is holistic, relational, experiential, reflective, and connected to identity, language, family, community, and the land (First Nations Education Steering Committee [FNESC], 2015). These principles challenged my previous understanding of teaching, which focused mainly on curriculum outcomes and assessment. I now recognize that meaningful learning also depends on relationships, belonging, and connections to place.

My understanding deepened further as I explored the Indian Act, Residential Schools, and the Sixties Scoop. Learning about these policies helped me recognize that many educational inequities experienced by Indigenous Peoples today are rooted in colonial systems rather than individual circumstances (Joseph, 2018; TRC, 2015). This understanding shifted my perspective from viewing reconciliation as teaching historical events to recognizing it as an ongoing commitment to addressing the impacts of colonialism through education.

Another important shift came from examining decolonization and Indigenization. I previously viewed these concepts as similar, but I now understand that decolonization involves challenging colonial assumptions embedded within education, while Indigenization creates meaningful space for Indigenous knowledge, languages, and pedagogies (Kulago et al., 2023; Queen's University Centre for Teaching and Learning, n.d.). This distinction changed how I think about curriculum planning by encouraging me to consider whose knowledge is represented and how Indigenous perspectives can become part of everyday learning.

Perhaps the greatest lesson from this inquiry is that reconciliation is fundamentally relational. Policies such as UNDRIP, DRIPA, and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Calls to Action provide important guidance, but meaningful reconciliation happens through respectful relationships among educators, students, families, and Indigenous communities (Government of British Columbia, 2022; United Nations, 2007). As a result, my focus has shifted from asking what Indigenous content I should teach to considering how I can create learning environments where Indigenous perspectives are valued and students become active participants in reconciliation.

This inquiry has therefore transformed not only my understanding of Indigenous education but also my identity as an educator. I now recognize that authentic integration begins with humility, self-reflection, and a commitment to lifelong learning alongside Indigenous communities.

03

Two-Eyed Seeing and the First Peoples Principles of Learning: Transforming Educational Practice

Learning

One of the most influential concepts explored throughout this course was Two-Eyed Seeing (Etuaptmumk), introduced by Mi'kmaw Elder Albert Marshall. Bartlett et al. (2012) describe Two-Eyed Seeing as learning to recognize the strengths of both Indigenous and Western knowledge systems and using them together for the benefit of all. At the beginning of this course, I viewed this concept as simply combining Indigenous and Western perspectives in classroom lessons. As my understanding deepened, I realized that Two-Eyed Seeing is not about merging two worldviews into one. Instead, it acknowledges that each knowledge system has its own history, values, and ways of understanding the world, and that both deserve equal respect.

This perspective challenged many of my assumptions about teaching and learning. Much of my previous educational experience emphasized measurable outcomes, scientific inquiry, and written evidence. While these approaches remain valuable, I now recognize that Indigenous ways of knowing also emphasize relationships, storytelling, observation, lived experiences, spirituality, and reciprocal connections with the land (Bartlett et al., 2012). Recognizing the strengths of both approaches allows educators to provide students with a richer and more balanced learning experience.

As an educator, I see Two-Eyed Seeing as a meaningful framework for curriculum planning. In science, students can investigate ecosystems through Western scientific inquiry while also learning about Indigenous ecological knowledge and responsibilities to care for the land. In mathematics, Indigenous patterns and designs can be explored not only for their mathematical concepts but also for their cultural significance. In social studies, historical documents can be studied alongside oral histories and community perspectives, helping students understand that knowledge can be preserved and shared in multiple ways. These examples demonstrate that Two-Eyed Seeing is not about replacing one worldview with another but about helping students appreciate the strengths of both.

Closely connected to Two-Eyed Seeing are the First Peoples Principles of Learning (FPPL), which became another foundation of my inquiry. The FPPL emphasize that learning is holistic, relational, experiential, reflective, and connected to identity, culture, language, family, community, and the land (First Nations Education Steering Committee [FNESC], 2015). Before this course, I viewed teaching primarily as delivering curriculum outcomes and assessing student achievement. The FPPL broadened my perspective by reminding me that meaningful learning also depends on relationships, belonging, and students' connections to place and community.

One principle that had a particularly strong impact on my thinking is that learning is embedded in memory, history, and story (FNESC, 2015). Previously, I considered storytelling to be an engaging teaching strategy. Through this course, I came to understand that storytelling is also a respected Indigenous pedagogy that preserves history, transmits knowledge, strengthens identity, and encourages learners to reflect on multiple perspectives. This understanding has expanded my view of literacy beyond written texts to include oral traditions and community knowledge.

Another important lesson was recognizing that learning should contribute to the well-being of individuals, families, communities, and the land (FNESC, 2015). This challenged my previous emphasis on academic achievement alone and encouraged me to think more holistically about assessment. Rather than relying solely on written work or tests, I now appreciate the value of reflection, collaboration, inquiry, and multiple ways for students to demonstrate their learning.

The course also deepened my understanding of inquiry-based and land-based learning. Inquiry encourages students to ask questions, investigate ideas, and construct understanding, while Indigenous inquiry emphasizes careful observation, listening, patience, and learning through relationships (Chrona, 2022). Similarly, I learned that land-based learning is much more than outdoor education. For Indigenous Peoples, the land is a living teacher that carries knowledge, language, history, and identity. Learning on the land helps students understand not only environmental concepts but also their responsibilities to care for the places where they live.

As an educator in British Columbia, I believe land-based learning also creates opportunities for students to connect with local First Nations and understand the ongoing relationships Indigenous communities have with their traditional territories. Rather than presenting Indigenous Peoples only within historical contexts, these experiences help students recognize Indigenous communities as living, resilient, and continually contributing to society.

Together, Two-Eyed Seeing, the First Peoples Principles of Learning, inquiry, and land-based learning have transformed my understanding of education. I no longer see Indigenous perspectives as additional curriculum content but as guiding principles that influence how learning is designed, how relationships are built, and how students develop respect for different ways of knowing. These frameworks have strengthened my commitment to creating learning experiences that are inclusive, meaningful, and supportive of reconciliation.

04

Decolonization, Indigenization, and Reconciliation: Moving Beyond Curriculum Inclusion

One of the most important lessons I learned throughout this course is that reconciliation requires more than including Indigenous content in classroom lessons. At the beginning of my inquiry, I believed that using Indigenous books or teaching about residential schools demonstrated a commitment to reconciliation. Although these practices are valuable, I now understand that authentic reconciliation requires educators to examine the colonial assumptions that continue to influence education and to create learning environments where Indigenous knowledge is valued equally alongside Western perspectives.

A major shift in my thinking came from understanding the difference between decolonization and Indigenization. Initially, I viewed these terms as interchangeable. However, I learned that decolonization involves recognizing and challenging colonial systems and assumptions that have historically marginalized Indigenous Peoples and their knowledge. Indigenization, in contrast, focuses on respectfully creating space for Indigenous voices, languages, cultures, and pedagogies within education (Queen's University Centre for Teaching and Learning, n.d.). Understanding this distinction changed how I think about curriculum. Rather than simply adding Indigenous activities to lessons, I now ask whose knowledge is represented, whose voices are missing, and how Indigenous perspectives can become part of everyday learning.

This understanding also helped me recognize the risks of tokenism. Kulago et al. (2023) argue that Indigenous content can appear superficial when it is added without changing underlying teaching practices. Throughout this course, I reflected on my own inquiry question and realized that meaningful reconciliation cannot be achieved through occasional Indigenous lessons or awareness days alone. Instead, Indigenous perspectives should shape how students learn, collaborate, ask questions, and build relationships throughout the school year.

Learning about Canada's colonial history further deepened my understanding of why reconciliation remains necessary. Before taking this course, I had limited knowledge of the Indian Act, Residential Schools, and the Sixties Scoop. Exploring these topics helped me understand that many of the inequities experienced by Indigenous Peoples today are rooted in colonial policies designed to suppress Indigenous languages, cultures, identities, and self-determination (Joseph, 2018; TRC, 2015). This learning shifted my perspective from viewing reconciliation as simply teaching history to recognizing it as addressing the ongoing impacts of colonialism through education.

Studying the Indian Act helped me understand how government legislation controlled many aspects of Indigenous life, including governance, education, and identity (Joseph, 2018). Similarly, learning about the Residential School System revealed how education was used as a tool of assimilation rather than empowerment (TRC, 2015). The Sixties Scoop further demonstrated that colonial policies extended beyond residential schools and continued disrupting Indigenous families and communities for generations (TRC, 2015). Together, these histories reinforced that educators have a responsibility to teach the truth while creating learning environments based on respect, inclusion, and reconciliation.

While understanding these historical injustices is essential, this course also emphasized the importance of recognizing Indigenous resilience, leadership, and self-determination. Reconciliation should not present Indigenous Peoples only through experiences of trauma. Throughout the course, I learned about Indigenous governance, language revitalization, environmental stewardship, healthcare leadership, literature, and artistic contributions. These examples shifted my thinking from a deficit-based perspective toward a strengths-based approach that recognizes Indigenous Peoples as leaders, innovators, educators, and community builders. Presenting both the impacts of colonialism and the ongoing achievements of Indigenous communities provides students with a more balanced and respectful understanding.

My inquiry also highlighted the importance of UNDRIP, British Columbia's Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act (DRIPA), and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Calls to Action, particularly Call to Action 62. Together, these documents emphasize that reconciliation is a shared responsibility and that education plays a central role in teaching Indigenous histories, rights, cultures, treaties, and contemporary contributions (Government of British Columbia, 2022; TRC, 2015; United Nations, 2007). I now understand that Call to Action 62 is not simply about adding more curriculum content; it encourages educators to create classrooms where Indigenous perspectives are integrated throughout learning and students develop empathy, critical thinking, and respect.

The National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation's Pathways: Five-Year Report further reinforced that although meaningful progress has been made in curriculum reform and public awareness, reconciliation requires ongoing accountability and commitment (NCTR, 2019). The report reminds educators that reconciliation is not the responsibility of Indigenous Peoples alone but of all Canadians. This message strengthened my understanding that my daily teaching practices contribute to Canada's broader journey toward reconciliation.

Overall, this section of my inquiry has transformed how I understand my role as an educator. Decolonization encourages me to question colonial assumptions, Indigenization challenges me to create meaningful space for Indigenous knowledge, and reconciliation calls me to build respectful relationships and foster inclusive learning environments. Together, these understandings have shifted my focus from simply teaching Indigenous content to creating educational experiences that honour Indigenous voices, support self-determination, and contribute to meaningful reconciliation.

05

Building Cultural Safety and Transforming My Classroom

One of the most meaningful shifts in my thinking throughout this course has been recognizing that reconciliation is not only about understanding the impacts of colonialism but also about celebrating the strength, resilience, and ongoing contributions of Indigenous Peoples. At the beginning of this course, I primarily associated Indigenous education with residential schools, treaties, and historical injustices. While these topics remain essential, I now understand that focusing only on trauma can unintentionally reinforce deficit-based perspectives. Students also need opportunities to learn about Indigenous excellence in education, healthcare, governance, literature, environmental stewardship, and the arts. This balanced approach helps students recognize Indigenous Peoples as leaders, innovators, and knowledge holders whose contributions continue to enrich Canadian society (TRC, 2015).

Learning about Indigenous leaders such as Dr. Nadine Caron, Dr. Alika Lafontaine, Christi Belcourt, Cherie Dimaline, and Dr. Michael Yellow Bird challenged many stereotypes and broadened my understanding of Indigenous leadership and resilience. These examples reminded me that reconciliation involves recognizing both the historical impacts of colonialism and the continued strength and achievements of Indigenous communities. As an educator, I want my students to appreciate Indigenous Peoples not only through historical events but also through their ongoing contributions to Canadian society.

Another concept that significantly influenced my thinking was Potlatch as Pedagogy. Davidson and Davidson (2018) explain that ceremonies such as the Potlatch are important educational systems through which knowledge, values, histories, and responsibilities are passed from one generation to another. This challenged my previous understanding of where learning takes place. I came to realize that education extends beyond classrooms and textbooks, with communities, ceremonies, storytelling, and lived experiences serving as valuable sources of knowledge. This understanding also reinforced the First Peoples Principles of Learning by highlighting that learning is relational, experiential, and connected to history, identity, and community (FNESC, 2015).

The course also strengthened my understanding of Indigenous self-government and self-determination. Learning about self-governing Nations demonstrated that reconciliation includes respecting Indigenous Peoples' authority to make decisions about their governance, education, languages, and cultures. This shifted my perspective from viewing Indigenous governance only as a historical topic to recognizing Indigenous Nations as active governments that continue to shape their communities today. As an educator, I want students to understand that Indigenous communities are contemporary, resilient, and continually contributing to Canada's future.

Another important area of learning was cultural safety. Before this course, I associated inclusion with creating welcoming classrooms. I now understand that cultural safety requires educators to recognize the ongoing impacts of colonialism, reflect on personal biases, challenge inequities, and ensure that Indigenous students and families feel respected and represented (National Collaborating Centre for Indigenous Health [NCCIH], 2017). Creating culturally safe classrooms also means listening to Indigenous voices, building respectful relationships with families and communities, and selecting authentic Indigenous resources.

The importance of using authentic Indigenous resources became another key lesson throughout this inquiry. The First Nations Education Steering Committee (2016) emphasizes that Indigenous perspectives should be represented through resources created by Indigenous authors, organizations, and Knowledge Keepers. Using authentic resources not only promotes accurate representation but also helps educators avoid stereotypes and tokenism. Throughout my teaching career, I intend to carefully select Indigenous-authored books, work with Indigenous Helping Teachers, Elders, and Knowledge Keepers, and connect learning to the local First Nations whose territories my students and I share.

Perhaps the greatest lesson from this course is that authentic integration begins with relationships rather than resources. Curriculum documents and lesson plans are important, but meaningful reconciliation develops through respectful partnerships with Indigenous communities. Learning about local First Nations has reminded me that every Nation has unique histories, languages, cultures, and ways of knowing. As an educator, I have a responsibility to continue learning from these communities and respectfully incorporate local perspectives into my teaching.

06

Implications for My Classroom

This inquiry has transformed how I plan to teach. Rather than limiting Indigenous education to specific units or awareness days, I will integrate Indigenous perspectives across the curriculum throughout the school year

In science, I will use Two-Eyed Seeing to combine Western scientific inquiry with Indigenous ecological knowledge. In mathematics, students will explore Indigenous patterns and designs while learning about their cultural significance. In English Language Arts, students will engage with authentic Indigenous literature, storytelling, and oral histories. In social studies, they will investigate treaties, Indigenous governance, self-determination, residential schools, and reconciliation while also learning about the resilience and ongoing contributions of local First Nations.

I will also incorporate inquiry-based and land-based learning, storytelling, collaborative discussions, reflective journals, art, and multimedia projects that provide multiple ways for students to demonstrate their learning. These approaches reflect both the First Peoples Principles of Learning and the BC curriculum's emphasis on inquiry, communication, and critical thinking (British Columbia Ministry of Education and Child Care, 2023).

More importantly, I want my classroom to be a place where reconciliation is experienced through everyday relationships rather than isolated lessons. I hope students will learn to respect Indigenous perspectives, challenge stereotypes, appreciate diverse ways of knowing, and recognize that they each have a role in building respectful relationships with Indigenous Peoples.

07

Conclusion

Returning to my inquiry question How can I authentically integrate Indigenous perspectives and Two-Eyed Seeing across the curriculum to support meaningful reconciliation? I now understand that there is no single strategy or lesson that fully answers it. Authentic integration requires ongoing learning, critical self-reflection, respectful relationships, and meaningful action.

Throughout this course, my understanding has evolved from viewing Indigenous education as curriculum content to recognizing it as a way of teaching, learning, and building relationships. Two-Eyed Seeing has shown me the value of honouring both Indigenous and Western knowledge systems, while the First Peoples Principles of Learning have reshaped my understanding of learning as holistic, relational, and connected to identity and place. Learning about decolonization, Indigenization, UNDRIP, DRIPA, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Calls to Action, and the Pathways: Five-Year Report has reinforced that reconciliation is both an educational and ethical responsibility.

Most importantly, I have learned that reconciliation begins with educators who remain lifelong learners. It requires humility, continuous reflection, and meaningful partnerships with Indigenous communities. As an educator, I am committed to creating a classroom where Indigenous voices are respected, authentic perspectives are integrated across the curriculum, and students develop the knowledge, empathy, and responsibility needed to contribute to meaningful reconciliation. Although this course marks the end of my inquiry, it also marks the beginning of my lifelong commitment to learning, listening, and supporting reconciliation through my teaching.
