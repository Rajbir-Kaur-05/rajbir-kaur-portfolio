Module 7

This module includes three key learnings and one overall reflection.

01

Pathways: Five-Year Report

Learning

From exploring the Pathways: Five-Year Report (2019), I learned that reconciliation is an ongoing journey that requires continuous action, accountability, and collaboration rather than a one-time initiative. The report highlights that while Canada has made progress in areas such as Indigenous education, language revitalization, and public awareness, many of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Calls to Action remain incomplete. I also learned that meaningful reconciliation depends on listening to Residential School Survivors, supporting Indigenous self-determination, and building respectful relationships with Indigenous communities (National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation [NCTR], 2019).

Why I Selected This

I selected this learning because it reinforced my understanding that reconciliation is a shared responsibility and that educators have an important role in supporting this work. Before exploring the Pathways Report, I understood the importance of teaching Indigenous histories, but this report helped me realize that meaningful reconciliation also requires sustained commitment, ongoing reflection, and action. I was especially inspired by the report's emphasis on learning from Indigenous voices, building respectful relationships, and recognizing Indigenous resilience alongside the impacts of colonization.

How I Will Apply This in My Classroom

  • Use authentic Indigenous-authored books and resources to ensure students learn from Indigenous perspectives.
  • Integrate Indigenous histories, cultures, languages, and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Calls to Action throughout the school year rather than as isolated lessons.
  • Encourage inquiry-based, land-based, and storytelling approaches that reflect the First Peoples Principles of Learning.
  • Help students explore the resilience, leadership, and ongoing contributions of Indigenous Peoples while learning about the historical impacts of residential schools and colonialism.
  • Invite local Elders, Knowledge Keepers, or Indigenous Helping Teachers to share their knowledge while following appropriate community protocols.
  • Create opportunities for students to demonstrate their learning through discussion, art, writing, presentations, and multimedia projects, fostering empathy, critical thinking, and respectful relationships.
02

Call to Action 62

Learning

From researching Truth and Reconciliation Commission Call to Action 62, I learned that education is one of the most powerful tools for advancing reconciliation in Canada. I learned that CTA 62 calls for all Kindergarten to Grade 12 students to receive age-appropriate education about residential schools, Treaties, and the histories and contributions of First Nations, Inuit, and Métis Peoples. I also learned that meaningful implementation requires well-prepared teachers, authentic Indigenous resources, strong partnerships with Indigenous communities, and equitable funding for First Nations schools. Although important progress has been made, CTA 62 remains "In Progress," highlighting that reconciliation through education is an ongoing responsibility (CBC News, n.d.; Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada [TRC], 2015).

Why I Selected This

I selected this learning because it reinforced my belief that education has the power to challenge stereotypes, promote understanding, and build respectful relationships. Before exploring CTA 62, I understood the importance of teaching Indigenous perspectives, but I did not fully appreciate how education itself is recognized as a key pathway toward reconciliation. Learning about the progress and remaining challenges of CTA 62 helped me recognize that reconciliation is not achieved through one lesson or special event but through a long-term commitment to integrating Indigenous perspectives authentically across the curriculum.

How I Will Apply This in My Classroom

  • Integrate Indigenous perspectives, histories, and ways of knowing throughout the school year rather than limiting them to special events or awareness days.
  • Use authentic resources created by Indigenous authors, Elders, Knowledge Keepers, and organizations to ensure respectful and accurate learning.
  • Incorporate land-based, inquiry-based, and storytelling approaches that align with the First Peoples Principles of Learning.
  • Teach students about residential schools, Treaties, and the resilience and ongoing contributions of First Nations, Inuit, and Métis Peoples using age-appropriate resources.
  • Build connections with local First Nations and incorporate local Indigenous knowledge whenever possible to make learning meaningful and relevant.
  • Create a classroom environment where students develop empathy, challenge stereotypes, appreciate diverse ways of knowing, and recognize their own role in advancing reconciliation.
03

UNDRIP and DRIPPA

Learning

From reading the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) and British Columbia's Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act (DRIPA) Action Plan (2022–2027), I learned that Indigenous Peoples have the right to self-determination, cultural preservation, language revitalization, education, and participation in decisions that affect their communities. I also learned that reconciliation requires more than acknowledging Indigenous rights; it requires governments, educators, and communities to take meaningful action to uphold these rights. The DRIPA Action Plan showed me how British Columbia is working to implement UNDRIP through actions that strengthen Indigenous leadership, improve cultural safety, and advance reconciliation across education and other sectors (Government of British Columbia, 2022; United Nations, 2007).

Why I Selected This

I selected this learning because it helped me understand that reconciliation is grounded in recognizing and respecting Indigenous rights rather than simply learning about Indigenous history. Before reading these documents, I viewed reconciliation mainly as an educational responsibility. Through UNDRIP and DRIPA, I realized that educators also have a role in supporting Indigenous rights by creating inclusive learning environments and teaching in ways that respect Indigenous knowledge, cultures, and perspectives. This learning reinforced that authentic reconciliation requires ongoing commitment, meaningful relationships, and action rather than symbolic gestures.

How I Will Apply This in My Classroom

  • Integrate Indigenous perspectives, histories, and ways of knowing throughout the school year rather than only during special events or awareness days.
  • Use authentic Indigenous resources and incorporate the First Peoples Principles of Learning to create meaningful and respectful learning experiences.
  • Teach students about Indigenous rights, Treaties, and reconciliation in age-appropriate ways while encouraging empathy, respect, and critical thinking.
  • Incorporate storytelling, inquiry-based learning, and land-based learning to help students develop meaningful connections with Indigenous perspectives.
  • Build respectful relationships with local First Nations communities and continue my own professional learning to ensure my teaching reflects authentic Indigenous voices and knowledge.
  • Create a culturally safe and inclusive classroom where all students feel respected and develop an appreciation for Indigenous cultures, languages, and contributions.
04

Overall Reflection

Module 5

These three learning experiences have deepened my understanding that reconciliation is an ongoing journey that requires commitment, meaningful relationships, and continuous action. Together, the Pathways: Five-Year Report, Truth and Reconciliation Commission Call to Action 62, and UNDRIP and DRIPA have helped me move beyond viewing reconciliation as simply teaching Indigenous history. Instead, I now understand that reconciliation involves recognizing Indigenous rights, listening to Indigenous voices, supporting self-determination, and creating learning environments that honour Indigenous knowledge, cultures, and perspectives (Government of British Columbia, 2022; National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation [NCTR], 2019; Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada [TRC], 2015; United Nations, 2007).

My first key takeaway came from the Pathways: Five-Year Report, which reminded me that reconciliation is a lifelong process rather than a destination. While the report acknowledges progress in education, language revitalization, and public awareness, it also emphasizes that many Calls to Action remain unfinished. This helped me recognize that educators have an ongoing responsibility to learn from Indigenous voices, build respectful relationships, and contribute to meaningful change through everyday teaching practices rather than isolated activities (NCTR, 2019).

My second key takeaway came from Truth and Reconciliation Commission Call to Action 62, which reinforced the essential role of education in advancing reconciliation. I learned that reconciliation begins with providing students with truthful, age-appropriate learning about residential schools, Treaties, and the histories and contributions of First Nations, Inuit, and Métis Peoples. This learning strengthened my belief that Indigenous perspectives should be integrated authentically across all areas of the curriculum so that students develop empathy, challenge stereotypes, and understand their own role in building respectful relationships (CBC News, n.d.; TRC, 2015).

My third key takeaway came from reading UNDRIP and the DRIPA Action Plan. These documents helped me understand that reconciliation is grounded in recognizing Indigenous rights and supporting Indigenous self-determination. I learned that educators also have a responsibility to respect these rights by creating culturally safe classrooms, using authentic Indigenous resources, and building meaningful partnerships with Indigenous communities. This shifted my thinking from viewing reconciliation primarily as teaching historical events to understanding it as an ongoing commitment to equity, respect, and justice (Government of British Columbia, 2022; United Nations, 2007).

Overall, these learnings have transformed my perspective as an educator. I now recognize that reconciliation should be embedded throughout everyday teaching rather than limited to special events or awareness days. By integrating Indigenous perspectives across subjects, using inquiry-based and land-based learning, following the First Peoples Principles of Learning, and continuing to build respectful relationships with local Indigenous communities, I hope to create a classroom where students learn the truth about Canada's history while also recognizing Indigenous resilience, leadership, and ongoing contributions. Most importantly, I want to help students become thoughtful, compassionate, and informed citizens who understand that reconciliation is a shared responsibility and a lifelong journey of learning and action.
