01

Resistance and Decolonization – From Dispossession to Dependency (Arthur Manuel)

Learning

From this module, I learned that the loss of Indigenous lands was not simply a historical event but the foundation of many of the challenges Indigenous communities continue to face today. Arthur Manuel explains that dispossession of land led to the loss of economic independence, governance, and self-determination, creating dependency through colonial policies such as the Indian Act. I also learned that reconciliation requires more than acknowledging the past; it involves recognizing Indigenous rights, supporting self-determination, and understanding that land is central to Indigenous identity, culture, and well-being (Manuel, 2015).

Why I Selected This

I selected this learning because it helped me understand the deep connection between land, identity, and self-determination from an Indigenous perspective. Before this module, I understood that colonization had harmful effects, but I had not fully realized how the loss of land continues to influence social, economic, and political realities today. Arthur Manuel's perspective challenged me to think critically about the lasting impacts of colonial policies and the importance of supporting Indigenous rights and meaningful reconciliation.

How I Will Apply This in My Classroom

  • Teach students that land is deeply connected to Indigenous identity, culture, and self-determination.
  • Use inquiry-based activities to help students explore how historical events continue to influence Indigenous communities today.
  • Encourage students to examine multiple perspectives when learning about Canadian history and reconciliation.
  • Create opportunities for respectful discussions about Indigenous rights, treaties, and self-determination using age-appropriate resources.
  • Foster empathy by helping students understand the lasting impacts of colonization while also recognizing Indigenous resilience and strengths.
  • Encourage students to think about ways they can contribute to respectful relationships and reconciliation within their school and community.

Classroom Activities

1. Traditional Territories Map Activity

  • Students explore a map of the traditional territories where they live
  • Discuss why land is important to Indigenous Peoples beyond ownership.
  • Reflect on the connection between land, culture, language, and identity.

    • 2. Cause-and-Effect Chart

    Students create a chart showing how colonial policies affected Indigenous communities.

    Colonial Policy Short-Term Impact Long-Term Impact
    Land dispossession Loss of land Loss of economic independence
    Indian Act Government control Dependency and reduced self-determination

    Students discuss how these impacts continue today.

    3. Inquiry Activity: "Why Does Land Matter?"

    Inquiry Question: Why is land important to Indigenous Peoples?

    Students Investigate:

    • Land and identity
    • Land and culture
    • Land and self-determination
    • Land stewardship

    Students share their learning through a poster or digital presentation.

    02

    Indigenous Activism: Art

    Learning

    rom this learning, I discovered that Indigenous art is much more than creative expression—it is a powerful form of storytelling, cultural preservation, and activism. I learned that Norval Morrisseau used his artwork to share Indigenous knowledge, spiritual teachings, and cultural identity while challenging colonial narratives. His painting Man Changing into Thunderbird symbolizes resilience, transformation, and the enduring strength of Indigenous Peoples. I also learned from Jeffery Hewitt that the artwork encourages Canadians to imagine transforming colonial systems into ones that recognize Indigenous rights, legal traditions, and sovereignty (Hewitt, 2017).

    Why I Selected This

    I selected this learning because it helped me understand how art can be a meaningful way to educate people about Indigenous histories and inspire reconciliation. Before this module, I viewed art mainly as a form of creative expression. Through Norval Morrisseau's work, I realized that art can also preserve culture, challenge injustice, and promote social change. This learning encouraged me to appreciate Indigenous perspectives and recognize the important role that artists play in strengthening cultural identity and inspiring dialogue (Art Canada Institute, n.d.; Hewitt, 2017).

    How I Will Apply This in My Classroom

    • Use authentic Indigenous artwork to introduce students to Indigenous histories, cultures, and perspectives.
    • Encourage students to explore the symbolism and messages in Indigenous art through observation, inquiry, and discussion.
    • Help students understand that art can be a powerful form of storytelling, cultural preservation, and activism.
    • Facilitate respectful conversations about Indigenous resilience, identity, and reconciliation using age-appropriate resources.
    • Encourage students to appreciate multiple perspectives and recognize the value of Indigenous ways of knowing.
    • Create opportunities for students to reflect on how art can inspire empathy, social responsibility, and positive change.

    Classroom Activities

    1. See–Think–Wonder

    Display: Man Changing into Thunderbird.

    Students respond to:

    • What do you see?
    • What do you think is happening?
    • What does this artwork make you wonder?

    Discuss how art can tell stories and communicate important messages.

    2. Symbolism Detective

  • Students identify symbols in Morrisseau's artwork (e.g., Thunderbird, colours, X-ray style).
  • In pairs, discuss what these symbols might represent and compare their ideas with the artist's intended meaning.
  • Reflect on how symbols can communicate cultural values and beliefs.

    • 3. Art as Activism Inquiry

    Inquiry Question: How can art inspire social change?

    • Students research one Indigenous artist and explain how their artwork shares stories, preserves culture, or promotes reconciliation.
    • Present findings through a poster, slideshow, or short oral presentation.
    03

    Self-Government and Sovereignty

    Learning

    From this learning, I gained a deeper understanding that Indigenous self-government is about restoring the right of Indigenous Nations to make decisions based on their own cultures, laws, values, and priorities. I learned that the Tla'amin Nation achieved self-government through the Tla'amin Final Agreement, which came into effect in 2016 after many years of treaty negotiations. Self-government has enabled the Nation to make decisions in areas such as education, land and natural resource management, language revitalization, health, citizenship, and community development while strengthening cultural identity and supporting reconciliation (Government of British Columbia, n.d.; Government of Canada, 2016; Tla'amin Nation, n.d.).

    Why I Selected This

    I selected this learning because it helped me understand that Indigenous Peoples are not only preserving their cultures but are also actively exercising leadership and self-governance today. Before exploring the Tla'amin Nation's journey, I had limited knowledge of modern treaties and how they support Indigenous self-determination. Learning about the Tla'amin Final Agreement showed me that reconciliation includes recognizing Indigenous rights, respecting nation-to-nation relationships, and supporting Indigenous communities as they determine their own future (BC Treaty Commission, n.d.; Government of Canada, 2016).

    How I Will Apply This in My Classroom

    • Teach students about modern treaties and Indigenous self-government using age-appropriate examples such as the Tla'amin Final Agreement.
    • Help students understand that Indigenous Nations continue to exercise leadership and make decisions that reflect their own cultures, values, and priorities.
    • Encourage students to explore the diversity of Indigenous Nations across British Columbia through inquiry-based learning.
    • Use authentic Indigenous resources to teach about governance, language revitalization, environmental stewardship, and reconciliation.
    • Foster respectful discussions about Indigenous rights, nation-to-nation relationships, and the importance of self-determination.
    • Encourage students to appreciate Indigenous perspectives and recognize the ongoing contributions of Indigenous communities to Canada.

    Classroom Activities

    1. Modern Treaty Investigation

    Students use age-appropriate resources to answer:

    • What is the Tla'amin Final Agreement?
    • When did it come into effect?
    • How has it benefited the Tla'amin Nation?
    • Why are modern treaties important for reconciliation?

    Students share one key learning with the class.

    2. Inquiry Activity: "What Does Self-Government Mean?"

    Inquiry Question: How does self-government strengthen Indigenous communities?

    Students investigate how the Tla'amin Nation governs areas such as:

    • Education
    • Language and culture
    • Land stewardship
    • Community well-being

    Students present their findings through a poster or slideshow.

    3. Reflection Circle

    Students reflect on:

    • What surprised you about the Tla'amin Nation?
    • Why is self-government important?
    • How does Indigenous self-government contribute to reconciliation?

    04

    Overall Reflection

    Module 5

    Module 5 has significantly deepened my understanding of Indigenous resistance, resilience, self-determination, and reconciliation. Each learning experience built upon the previous one, helping me move beyond viewing Indigenous history only through the lens of colonization to recognizing the strength, leadership, and ongoing contributions of Indigenous Peoples today (Art Canada Institute, n.d.; BC Treaty Commission, n.d.; Manuel, 2015).

    My first key takeaway came from Arthur Manuel's discussion of dispossession and dependency. I learned that the loss of Indigenous lands was not simply a historical event but continues to shape many social, economic, and political realities today. This learning helped me understand that land is inseparable from Indigenous identity, culture, governance, and self-determination. It also challenged me to think critically about reconciliation as an ongoing commitment to recognizing Indigenous rights rather than simply acknowledging historical injustices (Manuel, 2015).

    My second key takeaway was learning about Indigenous activism through art. Norval Morrisseau's Man Changing into Thunderbird showed me that art can be a powerful form of storytelling, cultural preservation, and resistance. I came to appreciate that Indigenous artists not only celebrate their cultures but also challenge colonial narratives and inspire social change. This learning reminded me that authentic Indigenous voices and creative expression are essential when teaching Indigenous histories and perspectives (Art Canada Institute, n.d.; Hewitt, 2017; The Canadian Encyclopedia, n.d.).

    My third key takeaway was exploring self-government and sovereignty through the Tla'amin Nation. Their journey demonstrated that Indigenous self-government is not only about political authority but also about restoring the ability of Indigenous communities to make decisions based on their own values, traditions, and priorities. Learning about the Tla'amin Final Agreement helped me recognize that modern treaties are living examples of reconciliation and nation-to-nation relationships in action (BC Treaty Commission, n.d.; Government of British Columbia, n.d.; Government of Canada, 2016; Tla'amin Nation, n.d.).

    Overall, this module has transformed my perspective as an educator. I now recognize the importance of teaching students about Indigenous resilience, leadership, activism, and self-determination alongside the historical impacts of colonization. Rather than presenting Indigenous Peoples only through experiences of oppression, I want my classroom to highlight their strengths, achievements, and ongoing contributions to Canadian society. By using authentic Indigenous resources, inquiry-based learning, and respectful discussions, I hope to create a classroom where students develop empathy, think critically about history, and build meaningful understanding of reconciliation and respectful nation-to-nation relationships.
